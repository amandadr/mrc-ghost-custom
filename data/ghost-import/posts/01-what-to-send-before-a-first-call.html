You do not need a finished brief to start a conversation. You need a clear picture of what is getting in the way.

People often arrive with a solution already picked. “We need Shopify.” “We need a new website.” “We need AI.” Sometimes that is right. Often it is a guess made under pressure. I would rather start with the problem.

If you only have ten minutes, send the URL, what is frustrating, and who currently updates it. That is enough. You can stop there.

If you only have ten minutes

  1. The URL of the site or tool — or a note that there is not one yet.
  2. What is frustrating, outdated, or harder than it should be. One paragraph is plenty.
  3. Who currently updates it, or whether nobody does.

That list is the whole brief. Everything else in this article is optional.

What is actually useful

A first conversation goes better when I can see the system you already have. Not a pitch deck. Not a wishlist of features. The thing people actually use.

The current site or tool. A link is better than a description. If customers look in more than one place — the website, a Google listing, a booking page — send those too. When those disagree, that disagreement is often the project.

What you are trying to get done. Book a table. Find Saturday hours. Apply for a program. Buy something. Call. If visitors cannot do that without phoning you, say so.

Who owns it after the work is done. If one person has the passwords, a former contractor does, or nobody is sure, that belongs in the first message. Ownership is not a later detail. It is the work.

Constraints that are real. A budget range, a season, a grant deadline, a board meeting, staff who are already stretched. I will not use those against you. I need them to recommend something you can actually run.

What you can skip

You do not need to name a platform. You do not need a sitemap. You do not need to collect every login.

Mood boards, plugin lists, and “a site like X” can wait. They are useful later. They are a poor substitute for “customers keep asking the same three questions.”

If you are not sure what the problem is called, that is normal. You do not have to know the technical name. Most projects here start with something that feels outdated, confusing, or harder than it should be.

What happens after you send it

You get a direct reply, not a sales sequence. Typical response time is one to two business days.

We talk about your organization, your customers, and what is actually in the way. I will tell you what I think needs to change, what can wait, and what you can do yourself without spending a cent.

If I am not the right fit, I will say so — and I will still try to point you somewhere useful.

What you can ignore

  • A twenty-page RFP for a first conversation.
  • Rebuilding the site before anyone has looked at it.
  • Hiring anyone to change the hours if you cannot change them yourself.
  • The feeling that you should already know what to ask for.

Start with what is frustrating. We can name it together.
