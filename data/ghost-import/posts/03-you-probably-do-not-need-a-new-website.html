The request I hear most often is a new website.

Sometimes that is the right work. Often it is the most expensive way to avoid a smaller problem: the site no longer matches the business, customers cannot take the next step, or nobody on the team can change a paragraph without a ticket.

The goal is not more technology. It is a business that works better with the technology it already depends on.

A new theme will not fix that. It can hide it for a season.

What usually needs attention

The information is wrong or hard to find. Services have changed. The booking path sits under three screens of atmosphere. The phone number in the footer is the old one.

The next step is unclear. People who are ready to buy, book, or ask still have to guess. That is not a branding problem.

The tools behind it do not connect. Forms go to an inbox nobody checks. Bookings are retyped into another system. The real hours live in a spreadsheet.

You cannot update it. If changing the hours requires a developer, the site is already more expensive than it looks — and it will be wrong by the first holiday.

I have sat with businesses whose site looked dated and whose actual leak was a form that died on a phone, or a homepage that disagreed with the listing people found first. We repaired those. We did not burn the house down.

When a rebuild is the honest answer

A rebuild is warranted when the structure itself cannot carry the work:

  • Staff cannot edit the pages that have to change.
  • The platform is abandoned, or so patched that every change is a risk.
  • Accessibility is not a missing plugin — the layout cannot work for keyboard or screen readers without starting over.
  • You are asking the site to do a different job (bookings, applications, a store) and the old one was a brochure.

Even then, rebuild the information architecture first. Decide what has to be true, who will update it, and what the next step is. The paint comes last.

A rebuild that leaves you unable to change a photo, a rate, or a typo is not finished. You should leave with the access, documentation, and a system you control — the same standard as a repair.

What you can ignore

  • A competitor’s new site as a reason to panic.
  • Plugin stacks that promise to transform a site you cannot maintain.
  • The idea that looking modern is the same as working.

If something is outdated, confusing, or taking more time than it should, start there. We can decide together whether the honest move is repair, a rebuild, or waiting.
