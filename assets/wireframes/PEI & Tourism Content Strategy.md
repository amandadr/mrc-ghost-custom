# PEI & Tourism Content Strategy

- Website Direction
    
    The site needs to do three jobs at once:
    
    1. **Reassure** small business owners that you understand their world.
    2. **Impress** organizational stakeholders that you can handle serious, complex work.
    3. **Equip** connectors—chambers, tourism groups, funders, referral partners—with useful things they can confidently pass along.
    
    That means the website should not feel like a portfolio. It should feel like a **trusted local resource centre with strong technical depth behind it**.
    
    ## The core experience
    
    When someone lands from an outreach email, the site should answer these questions almost immediately:
    
    - Does Manny work with businesses like mine?
    - Does Manny understand the kind of problem I have?
    - Is this going to be practical, or am I about to get sold an oversized solution?
    - Can I see examples?
    - Can I learn something useful before I contact anyone?
    - Is there an easy next step?
    
    For Atlantic Canadian audiences, especially local owner-operators and regional organizations, trust is likely to come less from aggressive credentials and more from **specificity, familiarity, usefulness, and evidence**.
    
    The feeling should be:
    
    > “They know the work, they understand this place, and they can clearly explain complicated things.”
    > 
    
    ## The site should be organized around people, not technology
    
    Your audience pages are the right move because they let visitors self-identify:
    
    - Small & Medium Businesses
    - Tourism & Hospitality
    - Arts, Culture & Community
    - Organizations & Institutions
    - Agencies & Development Teams
    
    Then your technical services sit underneath that as capability.
    
    That gives you two simultaneous paths through the site:
    
    **Audience-first**
    
    > “I run an inn. What can you do for me?”
    > 
    
    **Capability-first**
    
    > “We need a dashboard/integration/accessibility audit. Can you do that?”
    > 
    
    You need both.
    
    ## Think in layers of trust
    
    The best version of the site gradually increases confidence.
    
    ### Layer 1 — Recognition
    
    This is homepage and audience-page territory.
    
    Use language that sounds like the visitor's actual problems:
    
    - “Our website is out of date.”
    - “Customers keep asking the same questions.”
    - “Nobody knows how this system works anymore.”
    - “We need to get this done before the season starts.”
    
    The first reaction you want is:
    
    > “Oh, this is for me.”
    > 
    
    ### Layer 2 — Competence
    
    Then show breadth.
    
    Not giant skill inventories—evidence that you can handle the problem behind the problem:
    
    - websites
    - ecommerce
    - booking and ordering
    - hosting
    - analytics
    - accessibility
    - automation
    - AI
    - integrations
    - documentation
    - strategy
    
    The reaction becomes:
    
    > “Okay, this person actually knows what they're doing.”
    > 
    
    ### Layer 3 — Proof
    
    Case studies, project notes, examples, testimonials, and recognizable outcomes.
    
    For a small business:
    
    > fixed booking friction, improved mobile information, simplified operations.
    > 
    
    For an organization:
    
    > structured data, accessibility, governance, handoff, stakeholder coordination.
    > 
    
    The reaction becomes:
    
    > “They've done serious work.”
    > 
    
    ### Layer 4 — Generosity
    
    This is your guides/resources strategy.
    
    The site should give away enough genuinely useful knowledge that even someone who never hires you thinks:
    
    > “I'm saving this.”
    > 
    
    That is where your site starts doing business development without you being present.

    ## Design for forwarding
    
    This matters a lot for your target market.
    
    A Chamber advisor may not hire you.
    
    But they may send your guide to 15 businesses.
    
    A tourism manager may forward a checklist to their members.
    
    A consultant may send your article to a client.
    
    So every useful resource should be designed around the question:
    
    > “Would someone feel comfortable forwarding this with one sentence?”
    > 
    
    That means:
    
    - clear title
    - immediate usefulness
    - minimal sales copy
    - printable/downloadable where appropriate
    - your name and contact info visible but restrained
    - a relevant CTA at the end
    
    The content itself does the selling.
    
    ## Local credibility should be woven in, not shouted
    
    Do not make the site look like:
    
    > PEI PEI PEI PEI LOCAL WEB DESIGNER!!!
    > 
    
    That gets cheesy quickly.
    
    Instead, use regional familiarity naturally:
    
    - tourism seasons
    - Eastern PEI
    - local festivals
    - shoulder season
    - small teams
    - community organizations
    - owner-operated businesses
    - Atlantic Canada
    - rural operations
    
    Pair that with serious professional work.
    
    That combination is your differentiator:
    
    > **Local understanding + technical depth.**
    > 
    
    You do not want to look smaller because you're local.
    
    You want to look more useful because you're local.

    ## Make browsing rewarding
    
    You specifically said you want people to keep navigating through the shiny offerings.
    
    The trick is not more navigation.
    
    It's **contextual next steps**.
    
    At the bottom of a tourism guide:
    
    > Planning for next season?
    > 
    > 
    > Read the Tourism & Hospitality services page.
    > 
    
    On the Tourism page:
    
    > Preparing your website?
    > 
    > 
    > Download the Pre-Season Checklist.
    > 
    
    On the checklist:
    
    > Need help working through this?
    > 
    > 
    > Book a Digital Checkup.
    > 
    
    On a case study:
    
    > See how I approach maintainable systems.
    > 
    
    Every page should open one or two obvious doors.
    
    Not six.
    
    ## Your case studies should prove your method
    
    Do not make them portfolios of finished screenshots.
    
    Use them to demonstrate:
    
    - what the client needed
    - what constraints existed
    - what decisions you made
    - why
    - what improved
    - how ownership was handed back
    
    That is especially persuasive for organizational clients.
    
    Your Pictou County dashboard case study already supports this style well: it emphasizes dependable data sources, repeatable calculated metrics, maintainable publishing, documentation, and long-term ownership rather than simply showing a Tableau dashboard.

    It is:
    
    > “Here is what this technical thing actually means, what matters, and what you can ignore.”
    > 
    
    Your Core Web Vitals guide already follows that pattern by emphasizing interpretation, context, and useful signals rather than chasing scores.

    You can extend it into almost everything:
    
    - AI
    - SEO
    - hosting
    - accessibility
    - analytics
    - ecommerce
    - booking systems
    - automation
    
    Your brand already explicitly centres practical systems, clarity, measurable value, shared understanding, and avoiding unnecessary complexity.

    ## For local owner-operators, personality matters
    
    These users are often deciding:
    
    > “Do I want to deal with this person?”
    > 
    
    So your About page, tourism background, chip-truck story, PEI life, and straightforward tone genuinely matter.
    
    Not as decoration.
    
    As risk reduction.
    
    They need to believe you'll explain things without making them feel stupid, show up when you say you will, and not sell them a $10,000 system when $1,500 of work solves the problem.
    
    ## For organizations, process matters
    
    Different buyer.
    
    They need to see:
    
    - clear scope
    - stakeholder collaboration
    - accessibility
    - documentation
    - governance
    - data integrity
    - maintenance planning
    - professional deliverables
    
    So the site must remain credible enough that a municipal manager or economic development director can send it to leadership without saying:
    
    > “Ignore the folksy bits.”
    > 
    
    That's why the balance you've been building is valuable.
    
    Warm does not mean casual.
    
    Local does not mean amateur.

- Sorting: Website Component System
    
    Yes. I parsed all **14 HTML exports** as actual markup rather than treating the wireframes visually.
    
    The most important finding is that you should **not** recreate Relume’s component library one-for-one in Ghost. Across the exports there are **132 <section> blocks and 59 exact DOM section shapes**, but many are the same underlying pattern with different orientation, content density, buttons, imagery, or Relume component IDs. The audience pages alone repeatedly reuse the same tabbed feature, process, testimonial, project-list and resource-grid structures.
    
    I’d collapse those into a much smaller **MRC mother library**.
    
    # **1. Component architecture**
    
    I’d think about the theme at three levels:
    
    **Primitives → Section components → Page-specific compositions**
    
    For example:
    
    Button
    
    Media
    
    Meta tag
    
    Section intro
    
    ↓
    
    Feature Split
    
    ↓
    
    Tourism "Hospitality is different" section
    
    The *Tourism section* isn't necessarily a component. feature-split is.
    
    That distinction will save you a ton of CSS and maintenance.
    
    ---
    
    # **2. Foundation primitives**
    
    These aren't really Relume sections, but almost every component depends on them.
    
    | **ID** | **Primitive** | **Purpose** |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | P01 | section-shell | Standard outer section spacing, background treatment, width constraints |
    | P02 | section-header | Eyebrow + H2 + lead copy + optional action |
    | P03 | button | Primary / secondary / text-link variants |
    | P04 | button-group | Responsive grouping of 1–2 actions |
    | P05 | media | Image/video/embed wrapper with aspect-ratio variants |
    | P06 | meta-row | Category, resource type, read time, date, audience, etc. |
    | P07 | card-shell | Shared border/padding/background foundation for card-based components |
    
    I'd build these before recreating any Relume section.
    
    They should also consume your existing MRC design tokens instead of carrying across Relume's Tailwind assumptions.

    # **MRC Theme Redesign — Master Build Checklist**
    
    ## **Phase 0 — Protect what already works**
    
    Do this **before writing redesign CSS**.
    
    - Make a clean production theme backup from Ghost(Pro).
    - Commit the exact current production theme to Git.
    - Tag it something obvious, e.g. pre-2026-redesign.
    - Create a dedicated redesign branch, e.g. redesign-2026.
    - Confirm your local Ghost content/database is recent enough to expose real posts, tags, resources, pages, navigation, etc.
    - Capture screenshots of every current production template at desktop + mobile.
    - Record current Lighthouse/PageSpeed baselines for Home, Services, a post, and a case study.
    - Record the existing URLs that must survive the redesign.
    - Identify theme functionality that is **not part of the redesign** and should therefore be left alone.
    - Run the current theme through Ghost's validator before starting so you're not inheriting unidentified errors.
    - Do **not** delete existing components yet.
    
    ### **Deliverable**
    
    A safe rollback point.
    
    You should be able to type:
    
    “This redesign was a horrible idea.”
    
    …and restore the current site without reconstructing anything.
    
    ---
    
    # **Phase 1 — Audit the existing theme against the mother list**
    
    Before building new components, classify everything you already have.
    
    Use four statuses:
    
    **KEEP** — works and can survive substantially unchanged
    
    **EVOLVE** — existing implementation is useful but needs visual/structural upgrades
    
    **REPLACE** — same purpose exists, but new component should supersede it
    
    **NEW** — no equivalent exists
    
    Create a simple registry such as:
    
    | **ID** | **Component** | **Implementation** | **Existing?** | **Action** | **JS?** | **Priority** |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | G01 | Site Header | HBS + JS | Yes | EVOLVE | Yes | P0 |
    | G02 | Footer | HBS | Yes | EVOLVE | No | P0 |
    | P03 | Button | CSS | Yes | EVOLVE | No | P0 |
    | H01 | Hero Split | HBS/CSS pattern | Partial | REPLACE | No | P1 |
    | F01 | Feature Split | CSS pattern | Partial | EVOLVE | No | P1 |
    | I01 | Feature Tabs | HBS + JS | No | NEW | Yes | P1 |
    | D01 | Content Card | HBS partial | Yes-ish | EVOLVE | No | P1 |
    
    This prevents you from rebuilding things merely because Relume drew them differently.

    # **Phase 2 — Freeze the design foundations**
    
    Do this **before component layout work**.
    
    Your existing brand guide already establishes useful constraints: 17px default body copy, 16px minimum, 1.6–1.72 line height, approximately 72ch readable content width, serif headings, sans-serif UI/body copy, 600-weight emphasis, underlined links, and AA contrast.
    
    ## **2.1 Tokens**
    
    - Confirm colour tokens.
    - Confirm type families.
    - Confirm type scale.
    - Confirm spacing scale.

    - Add border styles.
    - Add radius values.
    - Add media aspect-ratio tokens.
    - Add transition-duration tokens.
    - Add focus-ring tokens.

    ## **Component definition-of-done**
    
    For **every component**, require:
    
    - 320px viewport works.
    - 375px works.
    - 768px works.
    - 1024px works.
    - 1280px+ works.
    - 200% browser zoom does not break layout.
    - No horizontal scrolling.
    - Keyboard focus remains visible.
    - Text remains readable without fixed heights.
    - Component tolerates longer headings than the wireframe.
    - Component tolerates missing optional fields.
    - No content is encoded in CSS.
    - No visual treatment depends on colour alone.
    - No JS is required for static content.
    
    This definition-of-done should become part of the project, not something you remember informally.

    # **Phase 18 — Deployment safety**
    
    Don't replace production blindly.
    
    - Build distributable theme ZIP.
    - Validate theme.
    - Upload as a **new theme**, not by overwriting your only known-good copy.
    - Preview it in Ghost.
    - Verify navigation against production content.
    - Check all key page templates.
    - Check a real article.
    - Check a real case study.
    - Check tags/archives if used.
    - Verify metadata/canonicals.
    - Run production Lighthouse.
    - Test contact form.
    - Activate only after smoke testing.
    - Keep old theme available for rollback.
